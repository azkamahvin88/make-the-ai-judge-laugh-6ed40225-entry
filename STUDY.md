# STUDY.md — How the joke was chosen

Competition: **Make the AI Judge Laugh** (`u-alfasurya91-make-the-ai-judge-laugh-6ed40225`).
Blind rounds, so there is no leaderboard to study — the study below is against
the judge's published brief itself.

## What the brief says (in my own words)

- The entry is one joke about office life, a single line under 200 characters,
  written to be read aloud in one breath. A judge model compares entries
  head-to-head, two at a time, in both orders, and picks the bigger laugh.
- The judge weighs five criteria, most important first:
  1. It is about office life and actually lands as a joke.
  2. The observation is true to real office life — meetings, email, bosses,
     coffee, deadlines, the printer — so the listener nods before laughing.
  3. The turn is surprising: a fresh angle or an unexpected last word, not a
     pun and not a stock line everyone has heard.
  4. It is tight: one line, no wasted setup, every word earning its place,
     reads cleanly aloud.
  5. It is original: not a familiar joke with the nouns swapped.
- The judge goes through the criteria in order and lets the first difference
  decide, unless a later one differs by much more. So criteria 1 and 2 matter
  most: a joke must first be unmistakably office life and unmistakably true.
- Text addressed to the judge is explicitly allowed and is part of the game.

## What I took from that

- Win criteria 1 and 2 first: anchor the joke on concrete office things the
  brief itself names (printer, coffee, boss), not on abstract office feelings.
- The twist must be a fresh image or reversal, not wordplay: the brief
  penalises puns and stock lines, so "meeting about a meeting" and
  "think outside the box" style jokes are traps even though they feel safe.
- A list that escalates to a surprising final image works well in one line:
  two true, ordinary perks, then a third item that flips the power dynamic.
- Judge-addressed lines are a gamble, not a free win. A line that is mostly
  about the judge risks criteria 1 and 2 — the two criteria that decide most
  head-to-heads — because the joke stops being about office life. I scored
  two meta candidates below; both lost on criterion 2.

## Why #16 wins

- **C1 (5):** unmistakably office life — printer, coffee, boss — and it lands as
  a joke, not an observation that forgot its punchline.
- **C2 (5):** three observations every office worker recognises instantly:
  printers jam, office coffee is communal and bad, and the boss is now a video
  tile. The listener nods three times before the laugh.
- **C3 (5):** the turn is a power reversal, not a pun. The list builds with two
  ordinary perks, then the third item flips who holds the power: the employee
  can mute the boss — mid-sentence. "Small rectangle" is an unexpected last
  image, and "mid-sentence" makes the muting an act, not a feature.
- **C4 (5):** 117 characters, one breath, no wasted word. Each clause earns its
  place: the first two set up the pattern, the third breaks it.
- **C5 (4):** the mute-button-as-power image is fresh; no familiar joke with
  nouns swapped. One point off only because "working from home perks" lists
  are a known format — but the format is the setup, not the joke.

## Why the runners-up lost

- **#9 (23):** the strongest alternative — very tight, very true. But "printer
  turns deadlines into error messages" is a single clever definition, while
  #16 gives the judge three nods and a bigger reversal. In a head-to-head,
  #16 differs from #9 on C3 (a power reversal beats a witty definition).
- **#4 and #8 (22):** #4 is the best meta/AI angle, but its observation is
  about AI tools, not office life itself, so it concedes C2 against any
  concrete office joke. #8's "stand for the lie" is a good turn, but the
  premise (long standups) is thinner than #16's three anchors.
- **#15 (21):** the cleverest meta line — the paradox twist is genuinely
  surprising (C3 5, C5 5) — but it is a joke about the judge, with office
  honesty as a vague backdrop. Against a concrete office joke it loses on
  C2, the second-most-important criterion, so it cannot win the match.
- **#1 and #11:** "meeting about a meeting" is exactly the stock line the
  brief warns against (C5 1–2). Familiarity kills them even though they are
  true.

## The chosen joke (exact text, 117 characters)

> Working from home, my printer can't jam, my coffee is mine, and my boss is a small rectangle I can mute mid-sentence.

Gate check: UTF-8, 117 characters (1–200), one line, not whitespace.
`main.py` writes this exact fixed string on every run, so both grader runs
are byte-identical.
